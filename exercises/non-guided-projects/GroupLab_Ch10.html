Chapter 10 Group Lab: Computer Vision with Google AI Studio

CAI1001C: Artificial Intelligence (AI) Thinking · Miami Dade College
What you're doing: You just saw YOLO detect objects using bounding boxes and fixed categories. Now you'll try a completely different approach — uploading images to Google AI Studio and asking a multimodal AI (Gemini) to describe what it sees. Instead of bounding boxes, you'll get natural language descriptions. Compare how these two approaches handle the same task: understanding images.

What you need: A laptop or phone with a browser. Go to aistudio.google.com and sign in with a Google account. Select a Gemini model (any available version is fine). Each group member should have 3–5 images ready on their phone or laptop (photos of everyday objects, food, places, etc.).

Submission: Complete this worksheet as a group. Type your answers in the fields below, then Print → Save as PDF and submit via Canvas.

Part 3: Testing Gemini's Limits

Every AI system has weaknesses. Let's find Gemini's. Each group member should try one of the following tests (divide them up).

Task 3.1: The Tricky Image Test

Upload an image that's challenging — blurry, taken from an unusual angle, dimly lit, or showing something uncommon. Ask Gemini to describe what it sees.

What made the image tricky? What did Gemini say? Was it accurate? What did it get wrong?
Task 3.2: The Impossible Question Test

Upload any image and ask Gemini something it can't possibly know from the image alone. Examples:

  • "What does this food taste like?"
  • "How old is this person?"
  • "What will happen next in this scene?"
  • "Who took this photo?"
Your impossible question What did Gemini say? Did it admit it couldn't know, or did it guess?
Task 3.3: The Text-in-Image Test

Upload an image that contains visible text — a sign, a menu, a book cover, a label. Ask Gemini: "What text can you see in this image?"

What text was actually in the image? What text did Gemini read? Was it accurate? Any errors?
🔗 Connection to Chapter Concepts: Remember the confidence threshold from the YOLO demo? YOLO at least gives you a number (e.g., 62% confidence) so you know when it's uncertain. Gemini often states things confidently even when guessing. Which approach is more honest about its limitations?

Wrap-Up Discussion

Your instructor will ask:

  1. Did any group find something Gemini was completely wrong about? What happened?
  2. When Gemini answered an "impossible question," did it admit uncertainty or state a guess as fact? Why does that matter?
  3. For a real-world application like airport security — would you want YOLO's structured bounding boxes or Gemini's natural language descriptions? Could you use both?
